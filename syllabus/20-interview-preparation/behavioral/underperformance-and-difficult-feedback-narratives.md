## Learning Objectives

After this chapter, you can tell an underperformance-and-difficult-feedback story that demonstrates a fair, specific, timely process — not just an outcome — and you can narrate one honestly even when the eventual result wasn't a clean turnaround, without the story sounding like a failure to tell it well.

## Why This Matters in Interviews

"Tell me about a time you had to give someone difficult feedback" is one of the most consistently asked Staff and lead-engineer behavioral questions, and it's specifically designed to surface whether a candidate has ever actually had this conversation, or has only ever told comfortable, positive-feedback stories. A candidate who deflects toward a mentoring story, or claims never to have needed to give difficult feedback, is themselves giving a real, negative signal — avoidance of the hard conversation is the most common real failure mode this question exists to detect, in the story itself, not just in the underlying behavior.

## Level 1 — Foundation

Picture the difference between a doctor who tells a patient "you're fine" because bad news is uncomfortable to deliver, and one who says, clearly and specifically, "your test results show X, and here's what we should do about it." The second doctor isn't being harsh — they're respecting the patient enough to give them the accurate information needed to actually address the problem. A strong underperformance story works the same way: it shows you delivering specific, accurate, timely information the other person needed, not withholding it to avoid an uncomfortable moment.

## Level 2 — Working Knowledge

At this level, the working signal to watch for in your own story is whether it's built around a *fair process*, not just a favorable outcome. A story where the outcome was an improvement is a good story; a story where the outcome was, honestly, that the person didn't improve and was eventually moved to a different role or off the team can be an equally strong story, if the process described — specific observations, a private and prompt conversation, a genuine chance to improve, a clear follow-up — was fair and well-executed throughout. Interviewers specifically probe for whether a candidate can tell this second kind of story honestly, because it's a real, common outcome and refusing to have one in your portfolio (per [Story Portfolio Design](02-story-portfolio-design.md)) is itself a gap.

## Mental Model: The Story Is About the Fair, Specific Process You Followed, Not Just Whether It Worked

The most common structural error in this story type is treating the *outcome* (did the person improve) as the entire measure of whether the story is a good one to tell. It isn't — a fair, specific, well-executed process that didn't produce a turnaround is a legitimate, tellable story, and a lucky improvement following a vague, avoidant, or unfair process is not actually a strong story, even if the outcome looks clean on the surface. Interviewers assessing this competency are evaluating your judgment and process, not the other person's eventual performance, which you don't fully control.

## The Underperformance Story Structure

| STAR component | Underperformance-specific content |
|---|---|
| Situation | The specific, recurring pattern observed (not a single bad week) — named concretely, with enough detail to show it was a real pattern, not a one-off |
| Task | The candidate's specific role and responsibility for addressing it — a peer noticing and raising it, a lead directly responsible, someone stepping in without formal authority |
| Action | The specific, fair process followed: a private and prompt conversation, leading with specific observed behavior rather than character judgment, asking what was going on before assuming a cause, agreeing on concrete next steps, setting a real follow-up date |
| Result | The actual outcome, honestly — a real, specific improvement with evidence, OR a genuine, fair process that didn't produce a turnaround, described honestly rather than glossed over |

The "asking what was going on before assuming a cause" detail in Action is a specific, credible signal: a candidate who describes only telling the person what to fix, with no genuine attempt to understand the actual cause first, is demonstrating a one-directional lecture, not a real feedback conversation.

## Illustrative Example

This example is illustrative — a representative scenario, not a real candidate's actual experience.

*"An engineer who'd joined our team about six months earlier and initially performed well started, over about three sprints, repeatedly submitting PRs that failed basic tests and missing stand-ups without notice. I noticed the pattern building over the second and third sprint, not just a single bad week, and since I was his direct lead, I scheduled a private conversation within a few days rather than waiting for our next scheduled 1:1. I opened with the three specific, dated instances rather than a general 'you've seemed off lately,' and then asked directly what had been going on, rather than assuming I already knew. It turned out he'd been dealing with a family health situation he hadn't disclosed to anyone on the team. We agreed together on a temporary, explicit reduction in his sprint commitment, and I set a two-week follow-up to check back specifically on how it was going, not just a vague 'let me know if you need anything.' At the two-week mark, his output had measurably recovered, and about a month later he mentioned, unprompted, that having the conversation directly rather than having it show up as a vague concern in his next performance review had actually made it easier for him to be honest about what was going on."*

## Distinguishing This from a Conflict Story or a Mentoring Story

This is not the same as a [Conflict and Technical Disagreement](06-conflict-and-technical-disagreement.md) story — that chapter covers disagreement over a technical decision between peers who are each performing their role well; this chapter covers a genuine, recurring performance pattern, a different and more sensitive category. It's also not the same as a [Mentoring and Developing Others](07-mentoring-and-developing-others.md) story — mentoring is proactive growth coaching for someone already performing adequately who wants to develop further; this chapter is about identifying and addressing an actual performance problem, a harder and more consequential conversation. Conflating the three produces a story that answers the wrong question when an interviewer specifically asks for this one.

## Interview Question: "Tell me about a time you had to give someone difficult feedback about their performance."

**What the interviewer is assessing:** whether the candidate has actually had this specific, uncomfortable conversation, has a real process for it, and can narrate an honest outcome rather than only a clean success story.

**Weak answer characteristics:** the candidate redirects to a mentoring or positive-feedback story instead; the "difficult feedback" described is actually mild or was delivered indirectly (through a manager, in a group setting, in writing only); the story never states what actually changed as a result, if anything.

**Strong answer structure:** S/T/A/R with Situation naming a specific, recurring pattern (not a single incident), Action describing a private, prompt, specific conversation with a genuine attempt to understand the cause, and Result stating the actual outcome honestly, including if it wasn't a clean turnaround.

**Staff-level expectations:** evidence of recognizing whether the underlying cause was individual or systemic (per [Handling Underperformance and Difficult Feedback Conversations](../../19-leadership-staff/handling-underperformance-and-difficult-feedback.md)'s Staff-level section) — did the candidate consider whether a broader team or process issue was actually behind what looked like one person's problem.

**Probing follow-ups:** "How did you know it was a real pattern and not just a rough patch?"; "What would you have done if the follow-up conversation showed no improvement at all?"; "Did you consider whether something systemic was contributing to this?"

**Self-review checklist:**
- [ ] The story describes a real, recurring pattern, not a single bad instance
- [ ] Action shows a specific, fair process — private, prompt, behavior-based, with a real attempt to understand the cause
- [ ] A concrete follow-up step is named, not just "we talked about it"
- [ ] Result is stated honestly, including if the outcome wasn't a full turnaround

## Common Mistakes

- Redirecting to a mentoring or positive-feedback story when specifically asked for a *difficult* one.
- Describing feedback delivered indirectly (through someone else, only in writing, only in a group setting) rather than a direct, private conversation.
- Leading with a character judgment ("they didn't care") rather than a specific, observed behavior.
- Only having a clean-success-story version prepared, with no honest account of a harder or less resolved outcome.

## Self-Review Checklist

- [ ] The pattern described is specific and recurring, not a single instance
- [ ] The conversation described was private, prompt, and led with observed behavior
- [ ] A real attempt to understand the underlying cause is shown, not just an assertion
- [ ] The Result is honest, including a partial or non-improvement outcome if that's what actually happened

## Summary

A strong underperformance-and-difficult-feedback story centers a fair, specific process — not just a favorable outcome you don't fully control. Distinguish it clearly from a conflict story (disagreement between two people each performing well) and a mentoring story (proactive growth coaching, not a performance problem). An honestly-told story where the outcome wasn't a clean turnaround, but the process was fair and well-executed throughout, is a legitimate and often stronger answer than a suspiciously tidy success story.
