## Learning Objectives

After this chapter, you can evaluate a job offer holistically rather than fixating on a single number, understand which parts of an offer are typically negotiable and which usually aren't, and negotiate professionally without burning goodwill with a team you may be about to join.

## Why This Matters

An offer is usually the culmination of weeks of interview preparation, and it's common for candidates to under-invest in the evaluation and negotiation step specifically because it feels like the hard part (the interviews) is already over. This is a mistake: a role's total value is determined as much by scope, team, growth trajectory, and level as by base compensation, and a poorly-evaluated or poorly-negotiated offer has real, compounding long-term cost — level and starting compensation frequently anchor future raises and later negotiations at other companies.

## A Note on Scope

This chapter covers evaluation frameworks and negotiation mechanics as general career-preparation knowledge. It is not financial, legal, or tax advice, and specific compensation structures, equity vesting terms, and negotiation norms vary meaningfully by company, level, location, and current market conditions — verify current specifics against the actual offer documents and, for anything with real financial or legal weight (equity terms, tax implications of a specific structure, contract clauses), consult a professional or the company's own HR/legal documentation rather than relying on general guidance alone.

## Level 1 — Foundation

Think about buying a used car by looking only at the sticker price. A cheaper car with unknown maintenance history, high mileage, and a shorter warranty can be worth meaningfully less than a pricier one with full service records and a longer warranty — the sticker number alone tells you almost nothing about the real value. A job offer works the same way: base salary is the sticker price, but level, team, growth trajectory, and equity vesting terms are the maintenance history and warranty — the details that actually determine whether the deal is good.

## Level 2 — Working Knowledge

At this level, the working discipline is the same one a careful car buyer applies before signing anything: check the details that don't show up on the sticker before committing, because level (like a car's maintenance history) is expensive and difficult to change after the fact — you don't get to renegotiate the mileage once you've driven off the lot. This chapter names exactly this: level is usually set during the interview loop itself, not negotiable after an offer, the same way a car's accumulated mileage isn't something a dealership can quietly reduce for you after the sale.

The working discipline for anything promised verbally — "we'll throw in new tires," "we'll match your other offer's equity refresh" — is the same one a careful buyer applies at the dealership: if it's not on the bill of sale, it didn't happen. A verbal promise that never makes it into the final written offer letter is exactly as enforceable as a verbal promise about tires that was never written into the car's sale contract — which is to say, not enforceable at all.

## Mental Model: Evaluate the Whole Offer, Not Just the Number

The most common evaluation mistake is comparing offers on base salary alone, or even total first-year compensation alone, while ignoring dimensions that materially affect the role's real value: the scope and growth trajectory of the position itself, the team and manager (who will shape day-to-day experience and career development far more than compensation structure), the level (which affects both current compensation bands and the trajectory for future promotions), and the vesting schedule and refresh policy for any equity component (a large headline equity number with a poor vesting structure or no refresh policy can be worth meaningfully less than a smaller number with better terms).

## What's Usually Negotiable, and What Usually Isn't

**Frequently negotiable:** base salary (within the company's band for the level), signing bonus, equity grant size, start date, and — less commonly discussed but sometimes available — remote/hybrid arrangement or specific team placement if the company is flexible about it.

**Frequently fixed or much harder to move:** the level itself (companies are often reluctant to change level post-interview-loop, since level determines the entire compensation band and was usually calibrated during the loop itself, not just at the offer stage), the overall compensation band structure (a company with strict banding may have real limits on what any individual negotiation can move, regardless of a candidate's specific ask), and standard benefits (health insurance, standard PTO policy) which are typically company-wide and not individually negotiated.

Because level is often the least negotiable but most consequential lever (it sets the band for everything else), the highest-leverage moment to influence it is usually *during* the interview loop itself — demonstrating Staff-level signal throughout the process, per the rest of this handbook — rather than attempting to argue for a higher level after an offer has already been extended at a specific level.

## Using Competing Offers Ethically

A genuine competing offer is one of the strongest and most legitimate levers in a negotiation — companies routinely account for this reality in their own process. Using it well means being honest about what the competing offer actually is; fabricating or exaggerating a competing offer is both an ethical problem and a practical risk, since recruiters occasionally verify or the exaggeration surfaces later. Being transparent about a real competing offer's specific terms is a normal, well-accepted negotiation practice — but note that expecting an exact match, rather than a good-faith consideration, is unrealistic, since companies have different bands, different equity structures, and different levels even for similar-sounding roles.

## Negotiation Mechanics

**Timing.** Negotiate after receiving a formal offer, not during the interview process itself — negotiating before an offer exists puts a candidate in a weaker position, since there's nothing concrete yet to negotiate against.

**Anchoring.** The first specific number stated in a negotiation tends to anchor the conversation; if asked for a number before you're ready to state one, it's reasonable to redirect ("I'd like to understand the full offer and the level's band before discussing a specific number") rather than anchoring low by guessing.

**Making the ask specific.** A vague "can you do better?" is harder for a recruiter to act on than a specific, justified ask — e.g., "based on [a genuine competing offer, market data, or a specific gap between the offer and the role's scope], I was hoping for X in base and Y in equity." Justifying the ask, briefly, tends to land better than an unexplained number.

**Professional tone throughout.** A negotiation is not adversarial by nature — the recruiter and hiring manager, in a healthy process, generally want to bring you on and are often more flexible than candidates assume, precisely because they've already invested significant time in the loop. An unnecessarily aggressive or ultimatum-heavy tone can create lasting friction with a team you're about to join, which is a real cost even if the negotiation technically "succeeds."

## Get It in Writing

Any change agreed to verbally (a revised number, a specific start date, a specific team commitment) should be reflected in the final written offer letter before acceptance — a verbal agreement that never makes it into the written offer is not enforceable and, in the worst case, can simply not materialize due to a miscommunication or a change in who's handling the paperwork. This is a purely mechanical, low-effort step worth never skipping.

## Common Mistakes

- Evaluating offers on base salary or first-year total comp alone, ignoring level, vesting terms, refresh policy, team, and growth trajectory.
- Fabricating or exaggerating a competing offer — an ethical problem and a practical risk if it surfaces.
- Attempting to negotiate level itself after an offer has already been extended, rather than recognizing level is usually set during the loop and is the least negotiable lever post-offer.
- Negotiating with an unnecessarily adversarial tone, creating friction with a team before even joining.
- Agreeing to changes verbally and not confirming them in the final written offer letter.

## Self-Review Checklist

- [ ] The offer is evaluated across level, scope, team, growth trajectory, and equity terms — not compensation alone
- [ ] Any competing offer cited in negotiation is genuine and represented accurately
- [ ] The negotiation ask is specific and briefly justified, not a vague "can you do better"
- [ ] The tone throughout is professional, not adversarial
- [ ] Every verbally-agreed change is confirmed in the final written offer before acceptance

## Summary

Evaluate an offer holistically — level, scope, team, growth trajectory, and equity terms, not compensation alone, since level in particular sets the band for nearly everything else and is usually the hardest lever to move after an offer has already been extended. A genuine competing offer is a legitimate and strong negotiation lever; fabricating one is both unethical and risky. Negotiate specific, briefly-justified asks professionally, and confirm every agreed change in the final written offer before accepting — a verbal agreement that never makes it into writing isn't enforceable.
