## Learning Objectives

After this chapter, you can prepare a small set of genuinely useful questions tailored to who you're actually talking to in each round, and use the answers to make a real, informed decision about the role — not just fill the obligatory "any questions for me?" slot at the end of an interview.

## Why This Matters in Interviews

This moment is frequently treated as a formality, but it's evaluated on both sides simultaneously: the interviewer is (often informally) assessing engagement and genuine curiosity through the quality of your questions, and — just as importantly — this is your actual opportunity to gather information you need to decide whether to accept an offer if one comes. Treating it as a box to check, with generic questions asked identically in every round, wastes both signals.

## Mental Model: This Is Real Diligence, Not a Ritual

The single most useful reframe: you are conducting your own diligence on this job, using a scarce and valuable resource — direct access to people who actually work there, in a context where they're incentivized to be candid (an interviewer trying to close a strong candidate usually answers honestly, since an obviously evasive answer to a reasonable question undermines their own goal of convincing you to join). Questions asked from this genuine-diligence mindset are naturally more specific and more interesting than questions asked purely to perform engagement — and they read as more engaged specifically because they *are* more engaged.

## Tailoring Questions to Who's Asking

The single highest-leverage adjustment: ask different questions depending on the interviewer's actual role, since each role has different, genuinely useful information to offer.

| Interviewer type | What they can answer that others can't |
|---|---|
| A peer engineer (same level, same or adjacent team) | The real day-to-day — what's the on-call load actually like, what's the code review culture, what's a recent technical decision they disagreed with and how it was resolved |
| Your prospective manager | Team priorities, how success is actually measured for this role, what happened to the last person in this role or a similar one |
| A skip-level or director | Org-level direction, how this team's work connects to company strategy, how the org handles cross-team prioritization conflicts |
| A recruiter or HR partner | Process mechanics, compensation structure, leveling — practical logistics more than technical or cultural substance |
| Someone from a team you'd work closely with, not join | Cross-team friction points, how dependencies actually get resourced and prioritized |

Asking a peer engineer a question that's really about org strategy, or asking a director a question about day-to-day code review norms, wastes the specific access each interviewer actually provides.

## A Working Question Bank by Interviewer Type

**For a peer engineer:** "What's a technical decision the team made recently that you disagreed with, and how did that get resolved?" (tests whether disagreement is handled well, and whether the interviewer will answer honestly); "What's the on-call rotation actually like — not the policy, the real experience last month?"; "What's the most annoying part of the codebase or process that hasn't been fixed yet, and why not?"

**For a prospective manager:** "What does success look like for this role at the six-month and one-year mark, concretely?"; "What happened to the last person in this role, or the closest equivalent?" (a manager who can't or won't answer this is itself informative); "How does the team decide what to prioritize when there's more good work than capacity?"

**For a skip-level or director:** "How does this team's roadmap connect to the broader org's priorities this year?"; "Where do you see the biggest risk to this team's plan for the next year?"

**For anyone, adaptable:** "What's changed about the team or the work in the last year that wouldn't be obvious from the outside?"; "What would make someone unsuccessful in this role, specifically — not a generic 'poor communication,' but something specific to this team or this work?"

## Questions Worth Avoiding

- Anything easily answered by the company's public website, careers page, or a five-minute search — asking it signals you didn't bother to look, the opposite of the engagement signal this moment is meant to demonstrate.
- Compensation or benefits questions in a technical or peer round — these belong with a recruiter or hiring manager specifically, and asking a peer engineer about equity often puts them in an uncomfortable position they're not positioned to answer well.
- Purely performative questions with no genuine informational content ("what do you love most about working here?") — usually produces a generic, low-information answer, and reads as filler rather than genuine curiosity.
- Adversarial or "gotcha" framed questions, even when the underlying concern is legitimate — a pointed but genuinely curious phrasing ("what's a technical decision you disagreed with, and how did it resolve?") gets more honest engagement than a confrontational one.

## Listening to the Answer, Not Just Asking the Question

The value of this exercise is in the answer, not the question — a candidate who asks a sharp question and then doesn't follow up or engage with what's actually said has wasted the opportunity. If an answer is evasive, generic, or visibly uncomfortable for the interviewer to give (especially to a direct, reasonable question like "what happened to the last person in this role"), that itself is real information worth weighing, not just noise to move past. Genuinely engaging with the answer — a brief, natural follow-up — also reinforces the engagement signal the question itself was meant to demonstrate.

## Common Mistakes

- Asking the identical, generic set of questions in every round regardless of who's answering, wasting the specific access each interviewer type provides.
- Questions easily answered by five minutes of public research — signals a lack of preparation, the opposite of the intended signal.
- Compensation questions directed at a peer engineer or technical interviewer, rather than a recruiter or hiring manager.
- Asking a question but not genuinely listening to or engaging with the answer.
- Having zero questions prepared — passing on this moment entirely reads as disengagement, even if unintentional.

## Self-Review Checklist

- [ ] Questions are tailored to what each specific interviewer type can uniquely answer
- [ ] None of the prepared questions are answerable from the company's own public materials
- [ ] Compensation and leveling questions are reserved for a recruiter or hiring manager round specifically
- [ ] At least one question is genuinely about the candidate's own real diligence needs, not performance of engagement
- [ ] There's a plan to actually engage with and remember the answers, not just ask and move on

## Summary

The "any questions for me?" moment is genuine two-way diligence, not a ritual — tailor questions to what each specific interviewer type can uniquely answer (a peer engineer's day-to-day reality, a manager's success criteria, a director's strategic context), avoid anything easily answered by public research or better directed at a recruiter, and genuinely engage with the answers rather than just collecting them. A candidate who treats this moment as real diligence both gathers more useful information and reads as more engaged, since the two outcomes come from the same underlying behavior.
