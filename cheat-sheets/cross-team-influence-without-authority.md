# Cross-Team Influence Without Authority

**Canonical chapter:** [`syllabus/19-leadership-staff/cross-team-influence-without-authority.md`](../syllabus/19-leadership-staff/cross-team-influence-without-authority.md)

## Core Mental Model

Influence without authority is a currency-exchange problem, not a persuasion-by-argument problem: people act on requests that offer them something they already value, and a technically correct case is necessary but rarely sufficient against a team with its own roadmap and incentives. Effort spent convincing the wrong person — someone merely Consulted or Informed rather than truly Accountable — converts nothing, no matter how persuasive.

## Essential Definitions

- **Influence without authority** (Cohen & Bradford) — getting someone to act differently with no formal power to require it; framed as identifying what the other party values and exchanging for it.
- **Team Topologies interaction modes** (Skelton & Pais) — *collaboration* (high-overhead, problem still being discovered), *X-as-a-Service* (consume a stable interface, low overhead), *facilitating* (temporarily helping another team gain a capability). A relationship stuck in collaboration long after the interface should have stabilized is a nameable, fixable problem.
- **Stakeholder mapping (RACI for a decision)** — identify who is Accountable (final say), Consulted (input matters, not final call), and Informed (notified after) for a *specific* decision, not a whole project.
- **Written proposal as influence tool** — lets objections surface as specific async comments rather than being lost in one meeting's dynamics (cf. Amazon's narrative-memo practice).
- **Reciprocity** — durable influence is built through prior unprompted help, not manufactured cold at the moment of the ask.

## Decision Table — Where to Invest Influence Effort

| Role in the decision | What it means | Where to invest effort |
|---|---|---|
| Accountable | Has final say over the specific change | Here — this is the actual persuasion target |
| Consulted | Input matters but doesn't decide | Secondary — gather input, don't mistake for a yes |
| Informed | Notified after the fact | Do not mistake enthusiastic agreement here for authorization |

## Common Pitfalls

- Investing persuasion effort in whoever is easiest to reach rather than whoever is actually Accountable — produces enthusiastic agreement from someone who can't authorize the change.
- Leading with the requester's own goal rather than the receiving team's stated priorities — technically correct, organizationally unpersuasive.
- Escalating on the first sign of friction instead of attempting direct engagement first — burns relationship capital faster than it resolves the disagreement.
- Treating a single meeting as sufficient for a nontrivial cross-team decision, with no written artifact stakeholders can review and return to.

## Interview Answer Skeleton

**30-sec:** Influence without authority means getting a team that doesn't report to you to act, by identifying the actual Accountable decision-maker and framing the ask around what they already care about — not around your own goal or whoever is easiest to reach.

**2-min:** Add the reframing mechanic with a concrete contrast: "adopt this library to reduce org-wide duplicate code" asks the other team to care about your metric; "adopting this library removes the three-day manual reconciliation task your team does every sprint" asks them to care about something they already wanted fixed. The second requires more upfront investigation but converts more reliably because it needs no translation on the receiving end. Escalation is a legitimate but costly tool — reserve it for after direct engagement has genuinely stalled, since overuse marks you as someone to route around.

**Whiteboard:** Draw a decision at the center with three labeled circles around it — Accountable, Consulted, Informed — and connect each to a named team or role. Circle Accountable and say: "this is the only circle persuasion effort actually moves."

**Staff-level framing:** At Staff scope, influence compounds into a track record that precedes any individual ask — delivering on cross-team commitments and using escalation sparingly builds default credibility, while a burned relationship makes every future proposal start from suspicion regardless of merit. Staff engineers are also positioned to notice when the same two teams keep needing ad hoc negotiation over the same kind of decision — a signal the interaction mode itself is wrong and should be redesigned (e.g., into a stable X-as-a-Service interface), not renegotiated from scratch each time.

## Warning Signs

- A cross-team proposal believed to be technically sound has stalled for weeks with no explicit "no," just diffuse non-response — check first whether it ever reached the actual Accountable owner rather than only someone Consulted or Informed; a technically sound but organizationally unmotivated ask often produces exactly this silent stall because no one feels empowered to say no.
