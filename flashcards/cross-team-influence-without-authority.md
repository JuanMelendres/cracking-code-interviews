# Flashcards: Cross-Team Influence Without Authority

**Canonical chapter:** [`syllabus/19-leadership-staff/cross-team-influence-without-authority.md`](../syllabus/19-leadership-staff/cross-team-influence-without-authority.md)

## Card: Influence as a currency exchange

**Prompt:**
How does *Influence Without Authority* (Cohen and Bradford) frame getting someone to act differently when you have no formal power over them?

**Answer:**
As a currency-exchange problem: people act on requests that offer them something they value in return, and the influencer's job is correctly identifying what the other party actually values — which is frequently not what the influencer assumes.

**Why it matters:**
A technically correct case for a change is necessary but often not sufficient; a team with its own roadmap and incentives won't reprioritize their quarter for a sound proposal that costs them time and offers nothing they were already trying to achieve.

**Common trap:**
Assuming persuasion by technical argument alone is enough, without investigating what the other team actually values.

## Card: Team Topologies interaction modes

**Prompt:**
Name the three interaction modes from Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais) for how teams work across a boundary.

**Answer:**
Collaboration (working closely together for a bounded period, high communication overhead, used while the problem is still being discovered), X-as-a-Service (one team consumes another's well-defined interface with minimal ongoing communication), and facilitating (one team temporarily helps another gain a capability).

**Why it matters:**
Naming which mode a relationship is actually in — and whether it's the right one — is a diagnostic tool: a relationship stuck in high-overhead collaboration long after the interface should have stabilized is a specific, nameable problem, not a vague "communication issue."

**Common trap:**
Treating chronic cross-team friction as a personality or communication problem instead of recognizing it as the wrong interaction mode for the relationship's current maturity.

## Card: RACI-style stakeholder mapping

**Prompt:**
Before investing effort to influence a decision, what should you map, and what's the common costly mistake?

**Answer:**
Who has genuine decision authority (Accountable), who is merely Consulted, and who is only Informed after the fact — applied to the specific decision, not a whole project. The common mistake is spending persuasion effort convincing someone who is only Informed while never engaging whoever is actually Accountable.

**Why it matters:**
A cross-team change routed to the correct Accountable owner from the start closes in far fewer round-trips than one that discovers, through trial and error, who actually needed convincing.

**Common trap:**
Investing effort in whoever is easiest to reach or most senior in general, rather than whoever is actually Accountable for this specific decision.

## Card: Framing around the receiving team's own priorities

**Prompt:**
Why does "adopting this library removes the three-day manual task your team does every sprint" convert better than "adopting this library reduces duplicate code across the org"?

**Answer:**
The second framing asks the receiving team to care about someone else's cross-org metric and requires them to do the translation themselves. The first asks them to care about something they already wanted fixed. It requires more upfront work from the influencer (understanding the other team's actual pain points) but converts more reliably because it needs no translation on the receiving end.

**Why it matters:**
This is the single highest-leverage reframe in cross-team influence — persuasion effort spent understanding the other team's priorities usually beats effort spent polishing your own pitch.

**Common trap:**
Leading with the requester's own goal rather than the receiving team's — technically correct, organizationally unpersuasive.

## Card: Reciprocity as the influence mechanism

**Prompt:**
Why is cross-team influence built entirely at the moment of an ask structurally weaker than influence built over time?

**Answer:**
Reciprocity underneath durable influence is a genuine exchange: an engineer who has previously helped another team unblock something, reviewed a design unasked, or shared useful context has built real standing that makes a future ask land differently than a cold request from someone with no prior relationship or track record.

**Why it matters:**
It explains why "building the relationship first through a smaller, unprompted contribution" is often the right move before a cold ask, especially with no existing relationship.

**Common trap:**
Leading with a cold ask to a team you've never helped or engaged with before, expecting the technical merit alone to carry it.

## Card: Escalation's compounding cost

**Prompt:**
Why is escalation to a shared manager a legitimate but costly tool, and when should it be used?

**Answer:**
Escalation works to force a decision, but each use spends relationship capital with the team being escalated over. A pattern of escalation-as-first-resort (rather than as a last resort after direct engagement has genuinely failed) marks an engineer as someone to route around rather than negotiate with.

**Why it matters:**
It's the opposite of durable influence — overuse damages the very reputation that makes future asks land with default credibility.

**Common trap:**
Escalating on the first sign of friction instead of attempting direct engagement first, or escalating a genuine resourcing problem as if it were a persuasion problem.
