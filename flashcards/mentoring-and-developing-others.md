# Flashcards: Mentoring and Developing Others

**Canonical chapter:** [`syllabus/19-leadership-staff/mentoring-and-developing-others.md`](../syllabus/19-leadership-staff/mentoring-and-developing-others.md)

## Card: Mentoring vs. sponsorship

**Prompt:**
What is the difference between mentoring and sponsorship, and why does the distinction matter for career advancement?

**Answer:**
Mentoring is coaching someone when you're together (developing their skills and judgment); sponsorship is advocating for someone's advancement when they're *not* in the room (naming them for a stretch project, promotion case, or visible opportunity). Research distinguishing the two (Herminia Ibarra) finds sponsorship often matters more for advancement than mentorship alone.

**Why it matters:**
The two are frequently conflated in casual usage, and a mentor who only coaches but never sponsors is providing only half of what actually advances someone's career.

**Common trap:**
Assuming mentoring alone (coaching conversations) is sufficient without ever advocating for the person when they aren't present.

## Card: GROW model structure

**Prompt:**
What does the GROW model stand for, and what is its core discipline?

**Answer:**
Goal, Reality, Options, Will — a coaching structure (John Whitmore) for a developmental conversation. Its core discipline is asking before telling: the mentor elicits the mentee's own goal, honest current state, options, and commitment, rather than supplying a solution unprompted.

**Why it matters:**
A mentor who immediately hands over their own answer produces a correct short-term outcome but denies the mentee the chance to develop the underlying judgment themselves.

**Common trap:**
Collapsing the conversation into the mentor doing the mentee's thinking for them ("giving the answer instead of asking the question").

## Card: SBI feedback model

**Prompt:**
What does SBI stand for, and why is it more actionable than a trait judgment like "you're not detail-oriented"?

**Answer:**
Situation, Behavior, Impact (Center for Creative Leadership). It structures feedback around a specific situation, the observable behavior in it, and its concrete impact — e.g., "in yesterday's incident review, the root-cause section didn't cite the log evidence, which meant a second re-investigation was needed." A trait judgment attacks identity and invites defensiveness; SBI describes an event the recipient can act on.

**Why it matters:**
Specificity, not bluntness, is the actual lever that makes feedback useful — honest-but-vague feedback is not more actionable than gentle-but-vague feedback.

**Common trap:**
Conflating "honest" with "actionable," or delivering feedback as a general trait characterization rather than a specific situation-behavior-impact chain.

## Card: The delegation-rung spectrum

**Prompt:**
Name the four rungs of the delegation spectrum, and what determines which rung to use for a given task.

**Answer:**
"Do exactly this," "propose a plan, I'll approve it," "keep me informed after you decide," and "full autonomy, tell me only if something changes materially" (adapted from Camille Fournier's *The Manager's Path*). The right rung depends on the person's demonstrated competence at that *specific task* — not their general seniority.

**Why it matters:**
Delegating at the wrong rung either smothers a capable person (too low) or abandons someone not yet ready (too high); treating a senior mentee as rung-4-for-everything ignores that competence is task-specific.

**Common trap:**
Delegating at a fixed rung regardless of the specific task, based on the person's overall seniority rather than their readiness for this particular piece of work.

## Card: Zone of proximal development

**Prompt:**
Where should a well-calibrated stretch assignment sit relative to what the mentee can already do?

**Answer:**
Inside the "zone of proximal development" (Lev Vygotsky) — the gap between what someone can do unaided and what they can do with the right support. Not below it (teaches nothing) and not above it (produces failure with no scaffolding to learn from).

**Why it matters:**
A correctly calibrated assignment is hard enough that the mentee must grow to succeed, but supported enough that failure is a recoverable, informative event rather than a costly one.

**Common trap:**
Assigning work the mentee can already do comfortably (no growth) or work far beyond any support structure (unrecoverable failure, no learning).

## Card: Productive struggle vs. a stall

**Prompt:**
How do you tell whether a mentee who's struggling with a task is building the skill or stalling on it?

**Answer:**
The signal is trajectory over time, not the current difficulty snapshot — genuine forward progress (just slower than the mentor would go) is productive struggle; being stuck on a wrong mental model with no convergence is a stall. Checking in at a fixed cadence, rather than only when asked, is what surfaces a stall early enough to correct cheaply.

**Why it matters:**
Confusing "being helpful" with "removing every obstacle instantly" prevents the productive-struggle zone from ever operating; but failing to catch a real stall wastes time on a wrong approach.

**Common trap:**
Judging by a single snapshot of visible difficulty instead of watching whether the mentee is actually converging over successive check-ins.
