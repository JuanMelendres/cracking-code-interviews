## Learning Objectives

After this chapter, you can take an existing story from your portfolio and deliberately reframe its emphasis to fit a specific company's named evaluation framework, without changing the underlying facts — and you understand why skipping this step is a common, avoidable reason strong candidates score poorly at companies that use one.

## Why This Matters in Interviews

Some companies (Amazon's Leadership Principles being the most well-known example, but far from the only one) interview against an explicit, named rubric that every interviewer is trained to score against directly — not an implicit sense of "was this a good story," but a specific, named principle the story is supposed to demonstrate. A candidate with an excellent story portfolio (see [Story Portfolio Design](02-story-portfolio-design.md)) can still score poorly at one of these companies if they never explicitly map their stories onto the company's actual named framework — the interviewer is listening for specific language and specific evidence tied to a specific principle, and a candidate who never engages with that framing leaves the interviewer to guess at the mapping themselves, which usually goes worse than doing it deliberately.

## Mental Model: A Lens, Not a New Set of Stories

This is the single most important thing to understand about this chapter: **company frameworks are a lens applied to your existing story portfolio, not a prompt to invent new stories.** The same story can usually be told with a different emphasis to fit different principles — a story about arguing for a technical-debt paydown (see [Technical Debt Advocacy](11-technical-debt-advocacy.md)) might, told with one emphasis, demonstrate "ownership," and told with a different emphasis, demonstrate "bias for action" or "have backbone, disagree and commit" — the underlying facts don't change, only which part of the story gets foregrounded. Building a completely separate set of stories per company is both unnecessary and a sign the portfolio-first approach (see [Story Portfolio Design](02-story-portfolio-design.md)) wasn't followed.

## The Amazon Leadership Principles as the Canonical Example

Amazon interviews explicitly against 16 named Leadership Principles (Customer Obsession, Ownership, Invent and Simplify, Are Right, A Lot, Learn and Be Curious, Hire and Develop the Best, Insist on the Highest Standards, Think Big, Bias for Action, Frugality, Earn Trust, Dive Deep, Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, Deliver Results, Strive to be Earth's Best Employer, Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility — the exact list and wording can change over time, so verify the current published list directly from the source rather than relying on a memorized copy). Every Amazon behavioral interviewer is trained to listen for evidence of specific principles and to probe with follow-ups specifically designed to test depth against that principle. This is the most widely-known instance of the pattern this chapter covers, which is why it's used as the worked example below — but the underlying reframing technique generalizes to any company with a named, explicit evaluation framework.

## The Reframing Method

For each principle in a target company's framework, ask: which existing story in my portfolio, told with which emphasis, demonstrates this specific principle most credibly? This is structurally the same exercise as the Senior-to-Staff scope reframing in [Scope, Impact, and Influence Framing](03-scope-impact-and-influence-framing.md) — the same underlying event, re-examined for a different dimension (principle-fit, rather than organizational scope) and re-told with a shifted emphasis, not a different set of facts.

```markdown
### <Principle name>
Candidate story (from your existing portfolio):
What emphasis shift makes this story credibly demonstrate this principle:
What would I need to add or foreground that the "default" telling of this story doesn't include:
```

Not every principle needs a fully distinct story — see the "two stories per principle" section below for how to think about coverage across the whole framework, and recognize that some overlap between a principle and an existing competency slot (see [Story Portfolio Design](02-story-portfolio-design.md)'s competency matrix) is expected and fine.

## Worked Example

This example is illustrative — a representative scenario, not a real candidate's actual experience — reusing the same underlying event as [Technical Debt Advocacy](11-technical-debt-advocacy.md)'s worked example, to demonstrate the reframing technique concretely (the same event, told with two different emphases).

**Default telling (as in the Technical Debt Advocacy chapter):** the story emphasizes translating a code-quality concern into a business-legible cost, and successfully persuading a skeptical stakeholder to prioritize a refactor over a competing feature.

**Reframed for "Ownership":** the same story, but foregrounding that the candidate noticed a worsening trend *before anyone asked them to look into it*, took personal responsibility for making the case even though it wasn't formally their decision to make, and stayed engaged through the following quarter's integrations to confirm the projected benefit actually materialized — "I didn't wait to be asked, and I didn't consider my job done once the pitch was approved; I tracked whether it actually worked."

**Reframed for "Bias for Action":** the same story, but foregrounding the two-week time-boxed nature of the proposed refactor and the decision to build a small, scoped proof rather than requesting a large, open-ended "let us refactor everything" allocation — "I deliberately scoped the ask to be small enough to say yes to quickly, rather than a large request that would have needed a much longer approval cycle."

**What changed in each reframe:** the underlying facts are identical in all three tellings; only which detail is foregrounded, and which sentence explicitly names the principle-relevant behavior, changes.

## The "Two Stories Per Principle" Expectation

Some frameworks (Amazon's LPs specifically) are known for expecting a candidate to have more than one available story per principle, since a single interview loop typically has multiple interviewers each probing several principles, and a candidate who visibly reuses the exact same story for two different principles across the loop (especially if the same interviewer notices, or if interviewers compare notes afterward) reads as having a thin portfolio. This doesn't mean literally writing 32 distinct stories for a 16-principle framework — it means, per the reframing method above, identifying at least two stories from your existing portfolio that could each independently demonstrate a given principle, so a repeated question in the loop doesn't force reusing the identical story with the identical emphasis.

## Bar-Raiser Calibration

Amazon specifically (and some companies with similar processes) includes a "bar raiser" — an interviewer from outside the immediate hiring team, trained specifically to maintain interview-quality consistency and empowered to veto a hire regardless of the rest of the panel's opinion. The practical implication for a candidate: the bar raiser is often the most rigorously trained on the framework itself and the most likely to probe deeply on a specific principle with pointed follow-ups, so the depth of preparation described in this chapter matters most for whichever round turns out to be with the bar raiser — though a candidate typically doesn't know in advance which interviewer holds that role.

## Beyond Amazon: Recognizing the Pattern Elsewhere

Many companies without a numbered, publicly-documented framework as explicit as Amazon's LPs still interview against an internal, less-publicized set of values or competencies. The signal that a company is using this pattern: job postings or careers pages that name specific values with capitalized titles, interviewers who ask questions with unusually specific, consistent phrasing across a loop, or explicit mention during a recruiter screen of "we evaluate against our core values." When this pattern is detected, the same reframing method applies — research the company's actual stated values (from their own published materials, not a third party's guess) and apply the same per-principle reframing exercise described above.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating this as a prompt to write an entirely new set of stories, rather than reframing the existing portfolio — wasteful and usually produces thinner material than the reframing approach.
- Reciting a principle's name without genuinely demonstrating it — an interviewer trained on the framework can tell the difference between a story that organically demonstrates "ownership" and one that awkwardly inserts the word "ownership" into an unrelated narrative.
- Reusing the identical story, with the identical emphasis, for two different principles in the same loop — visible to interviewers who compare notes, and reads as a thin portfolio.
- Memorizing a stale or third-party-summarized version of a company's framework rather than the company's own current, official wording — frameworks are occasionally revised, and citing an outdated principle name is a small but noticeable tell.

## Self-Review Checklist

- [ ] For each target principle, at least one existing portfolio story is mapped to it with a genuine, specific emphasis shift — not a name-drop
- [ ] At least the highest-priority principles have two independently-usable stories, per the "two stories per principle" norm
- [ ] The company's framework was verified against their own current published materials, not a memorized or third-party summary
- [ ] The reframed tellings don't contradict the story's default telling — they're an emphasis shift on the same real facts, not a different story

## Summary

Company-specific frameworks are a lens applied to an existing story portfolio, not a prompt to build a separate set of stories — the same underlying event can usually be reframed with a different emphasis to credibly demonstrate different named principles, exactly as [Scope, Impact, and Influence Framing](03-scope-impact-and-influence-framing.md)'s technique reframes the same event for organizational scope instead. Verify a target company's actual current framework from their own materials, prepare at least two stories per high-priority principle to avoid visible repetition across a loop, and remember that reciting a principle's name is not the same as genuinely demonstrating it.
